The idea in short

A short animated video: the problem, the six cones and the 5 − ε construction.

1Six cones, one arrow per cone

Think of a road network between cities. A road between every pair of cities is far too expensive. So we keep only a few roads. But we still want every trip to be short: never much longer than the straight line. A graph like this is called a spanner. Its worst detour factor is its spanning ratio. Θ-graphs are one of the simplest ways to build one: each city only builds a road to its nearest neighbour in a few directions.

Now make every road one-way. You can still leave a city in every direction. But the road back may not exist and a trip may have to go around. This is the directed Θ6-graph. The paper shows that the detour can get as bad as 5 times the straight line but never worse.

Let \(P\) be a finite set of points in the plane. The three lines of slope \(-\sqrt3\), \(0\) and \(\sqrt3\) through a point \(u\) cut the plane into six cones of 60°, numbered \(C_u^0,\dots,C_u^5\) counter-clockwise, starting with \(C_u^0\) directly below \(u\). In each non-empty cone, \(u\) gets one outgoing edge: to the point whose orthogonal projection on the bisector of the cone is closest to \(u\). Θ-graphs were introduced by Clarkson [6] and by Keil and Gutwin [7].

Equivalently, \(u\to v\) is an edge exactly when the canonical triangle \(\nabla_u^v\) (the equilateral triangle with a corner at \(u\), sides parallel to the cone boundaries and \(v\) on the opposite side) contains no point of \(P\) in its interior. The far side of \(\nabla_u^v\) is exactly the level line of the projection. So the two definitions are the same. My code builds the graph both ways and checks that they agree (§6).

Every vertex has out-degree at most 6 but its in-degree can be large. The spanning ratio is

$$\rho(G) = \max_{s\neq t\in P} \frac{d_G(s,t)}{|st|},$$

where \(d_G\) is the length of a shortest directed path. As in the paper, I assume general position: no two points on a line of slope \(0\) or \(\pm\sqrt3\). So no point lies on a cone boundary.

How it is computed

Brute force: for each \(u\), the closest projection in each cone, \(O(n^2)\). The exact spanning ratio runs Dijkstra from every vertex on the \(\le 6n\) edges, \(O(n^2\log n)\). This is instant for the sizes used here. That is why I did not implement the \(O(n\log n)\) sweep of [8].

Greedy routing

From the current vertex \(a\), take the out-edge in the cone of \(a\) that contains \(t\). This edge always exists because \(t\) itself is in that cone. In Θ6 the greedy path can spiral around \(t\) many times while making little progress [2, 3] (see Greedy routing can spiral). Bounded greedy routing is only known for \(k\ge 7\) cones [9].

Things to try

Load 5 points ≈ 4: the points \(s,a,b,c,t\) of the construction in §3, without the zig-zag, already give 3.966. The ratio tends to 4 as δ → 0. Switch to undirected and see the ratio fall under 2. Compare the greedy path and the shortest path on random sets: they often differ.

Greedy routing can spiral

Greedy routing looks like the natural way to route in Θ6 but it can spiral around \(t\) with a path of unbounded length ([3], p. 4 and Fig. 2, taken from an earlier paper; see also [2, p. 3]). I built my own example with the code. The points \(a_0=s, a_1, a_2,\dots\) lie on a "hexagonal spiral" around \(t\): \(a_{k+1}\) is \(a_k\) turned by 60° around \(t\) and moved closer to \(t\) by a factor \(\rho\).

Why it works: seen from \(a_k\), the point \(t\) lies on the far side of the canonical triangle \(\nabla_{a_k}^t\), close to one end. The point \(a_{k+1}\) lies inside the triangle near the other end. So \(a_{k+1}\) has a smaller projection than \(t\) and greedy goes there. Then the same picture repeats, turned by 60°.

With 25 points greedy makes 4 full turns before reaching \(t\). In this example the shortest path is the same spiral (I checked it with Dijkstra, see §6). Its length still stays small: each turn multiplies the distance to \(t\) by \(\rho^6\). The lengths form a geometric series (the same kind of argument as for the zig-zag in §3). For \(\rho\) above about 0.73 (with the starting angle I use) it stops working: \(a_{k+6}\), one turn later, falls inside \(\nabla_{a_k}^t\) with a smaller projection than \(a_{k+1}\). Then greedy jumps directly one turn inwards.

3Rebuilding the 5 − ε construction

Lemma 1 of the paper: for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a point set whose directed Θ6-graph has spanning ratio at least \(5-\varepsilon\). Fix \(0<\delta<\varepsilon/72\), \(t=(0,0)\) and put \(s, a, b, c\) close to four corners of the unit hexagon around \(t\):

$$\begin{aligned} s&=(-1,0)+\delta(1,-1), & a&=(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})+\delta(1,-2),\\ b&=(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})+\delta(-2,-3), & c&=(1,0)+\delta(-6,1). \end{aligned}$$

Then two almost parallel lines through \(t\) are added: the points \(p^0,p^1,\dots,p^k\) on the line of slope \(-\delta\) and \(q^0,\dots,q^{k-1}\) on the segment \(ct\). Consecutive points are linked by segments of slope \(\pm(\sqrt3-\delta)\). They form a zig-zag that gets smaller towards \(t\). From each vertex of the path, all later vertices are in the same cone. So the shortest path from \(s\) to \(t\) is

s → a → b → c → p⁰ → q⁰ → p¹ → q¹ → ⋯ → pᵏ → t.

The first three edges have length ≈ 1 each. Unfolding the zig-zag gives the key step: its length equals \(|p^0r|+|rp^k|\), where \(r\) is the point where the line of slope \(\sqrt3-\delta\) through \(p^k\) meets the line of slope \(\delta-\sqrt3\) through \(p^0\). When δ → 0, \(r\) tends to \((\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})\). So the zig-zag is as long as going from \(c\) back up to the top of the hexagon and down again to \(t\). In total this is ≈ 5 and \(|st|\le 1\). The paper bounds the total from below by \(5-72\delta\).

What the numbers say

For every δ I tried, Dijkstra finds exactly the path described in the paper and the ratio is above \(5-72\delta\). The measured gap is about \(5-\text{ratio}\approx 18\,\delta\) (for example 4.9818 at δ = 0.001, with 5 962 points). The constant 72 of the paper is therefore not tight. This is expected because each length is bounded separately.

The number of points grows like \(1/\delta\): the zig-zag steps shrink geometrically towards \(t\) and the construction stops when \(p^k\) gets closer to \(t\) than δ. Approaching 5 is expensive. By contrast, 5 points (\(s,a,b,c,t\)) already give a ratio close to 4 (see the 5-point example just after).

Implementation note: a probable typo in the paper. Both the full version [2] and the SoCG version [1] (p. 5 in both) define the points \(q^i\) by \(\|q^{i-1}q^i\|_2/\|tq^i\|_2 = \|q^0c\|_2/\|tc\|_2\). Built literally, this point set has the wrong shortest path: it jumps \(p^0\to p^1\to\cdots\), skips about half of the points and the ratio stays below 4 (3.95 at \(\delta=0.005\)). With the denominator \(\|tq^{i-1}\|_2\) instead, \((q^{i-1},p^i,q^i)\) is a scaled copy of \((c,p^0,q^0)\), the formula also holds for \(i=0\) (with \(q^{-1}=c\)) and the shortest path is exactly the one of Figure 2 of the paper. This page uses that version. My test suite compares both. With this index the construction works. So I think it is only a typo. I have not asked the authors.

A smaller example: 4 − ε with 5 points

The first construction with ratio \(4-\varepsilon\) is in [3] (Theorem 25): 8 points placed near the corners and the center of a hexagon. A simpler one comes for free from Lemma 1: keep only \(s,a,b,c,t\). The shortest path from \(s\) to \(t\) is then \(s\to a\to b\to c\to t\), four edges of length ≈ 1 while \(|st|\approx 1\). Numerically the ratio is about \(4-11.3\,\delta\) (3.9887 for δ = 0.001). Move δ in the figure to see it; it is also the preset 5 points ≈ 4 of the lab. It is not the construction of [3] with its 8 points.

Why 4: the path makes three sides of the hexagon around \(t\) (\(s\to a\to b\to c\)) and then goes straight from \(c\) to \(t\). So it has length close to 4 while \(s\) is at distance close to 1 from \(t\). The zig-zag of Lemma 1 is exactly what replaces the last edge \(c\to t\) by a path of length close to 2.

First results of the random search

I wrote a simulated-annealing search: it moves one point at a time and accepts worse moves with a probability that decreases over time. Moves that bring two points within \(10^{-3}\) of each other, or within \(10^{-3}\) rad of a cone boundary, are rejected. Without this rule, the search exploits degenerate configurations (points on the same horizontal line) and reports meaningless ratios in the thousands. The best sets were checked with both edge definitions. For the final version half of the restarts start from the points \(s,a,b,c,t\) of Lemma 1 (plus random points near \(ct\)) instead of random points. Best ratio found, 8 restarts × 6 000 steps:

5Why never more than 5: a map of the proof

The paper proves something stronger than \(d(s,t)\le 5|st|\):

Theorem 1. For every \(P\) in general position and all \(s,t\in P\): \(d_{\Theta_6}(s,t)\le 5\|st\|_{\tipn}\le 5|st|\).

The idea is to measure distances with hexagons instead of circles because hexagons fit the six cones. Here \(\|\cdot\|_{\hexn}\) is the hexagonal norm whose unit ball is the regular hexagon with corners \((\pm1,0)\) (it defines Θ6). The other one, \(\|\cdot\|_{\tipn}\), is the "tipped" hexagonal norm whose unit ball has corners \((0,\pm 2/\sqrt3)\). We have \(\|\cdot\|_{\tipn}\le\|\cdot\|_2\le\|\cdot\|_{\hexn}\).

The proof goes in four steps. Each step has its own block, with its own interactive figure.

  1. 5.1 The target and the empty region: induction on the distance, the target length \(5-2.5y_0\) (Lemma 2), and a region \(R\) that a counterexample forces to be empty (Lemma 4).
  2. 5.2 The toll for crossing \(R\): greedy paths can spiral, so the proof stops them at the first edge that jumps over \(R\). Lemma 9 says where such an edge lands.
  3. 5.3 Two candidate paths: one path on each side of \(t\). If both were too long, the points would satisfy a list of inequalities.
  4. 5.4 The linear programs: these inequalities never hold together. Linear programming rules out 16 of the 20 cases and a third path closes the other 4.

5.1 The target and the empty region

Induction. Assume the bound holds for every pair that is closer than \(s\) and \(t\) in \(\|\cdot\|_{\hexn}\). Then look for a short enough path from \(s\) to \(t\). The closest pair is a direct edge. Normalise \(\|st\|_{\hexn}=1\) and, by symmetry, \(s\in C_t^0\) to the right of the bisector \(B_t^0\).

The target (Lemma 2). The target length can be read off one small triangle between \(s\) and \(t\): the bigger the triangle, the smaller the target. Let \(y_0\) be the side of the triangle \(T=C_t^0\cap C_s^2\). Then \(1=\|st\|_{\tipn}+\tfrac12 y_0\). So the target is \(5\|st\|_{\tipn}=5-2.5\,y_0\). checked in §6

The empty region (Definition 1, Lemma 4). If \(s,t\) were a counterexample, a whole region between them would have to be empty. And the arrow leaving \(s\) upwards would have to land in a small window. Let \(p=B_s^3\cap B_t^1\) and \(q=B_s^4\cap B_t^0\), and let \(R\) be the parallelogram \(sqtp\) together with \(\nabla_s^p\) and \(\nabla_s^q\). If \(d(s,t)>5\|st\|_{\tipn}\), then no point is inside \(R\), the edge \(sy\) of \(C_s^3\) ends in \(C_t^1\), and \(2.5y_0+3.5y_1<1\), where \(y_1=1-\|\nabla_s^y\|\).

The figure shows \(R\), the triangle \(T\) and the window where \(y\) would have to be.

Details: why the region \(R\) is empty

The parallelogram \(Q=sqtp\) is exactly the region where going through a point costs nothing extra in the tipped norm: for every \(w\in Q\), \(\|sw\|_{\tipn}+\|wt\|_{\tipn}=\|st\|_{\tipn}\) (checked in §6). If a vertex \(w\ne s,t\) were in \(Q\), both pieces would be shorter than \(st\), so induction would give

$$d(s,t)\le d(s,w)+d(w,t)\le 5\|sw\|_{\tipn}+5\|wt\|_{\tipn}=5\|st\|_{\tipn},$$

and \(s,t\) would not be a counterexample ([2], p. 9). For the two triangles \(\nabla_s^p\) and \(\nabla_s^q\) the paper uses Lemma 3 instead (pp. 10–11), also checked in §6:

Lemma 3. If \(u,v\in C_t^i\cup C_t^{i+1}\) lie between the bisectors \(B_t^i\) and \(B_t^{i+1}\), and \(v\in C_u^{i+3}\cup C_u^{i+4}\), then \(\|uv\|_{\hexn}\le 2(\|ut\|_{\tipn}-\|vt\|_{\tipn})\).

Details: where inequality (2) comes from

This is the one inequality of the linear programs that I re-derive here (from [2], pp. 10–11). Assume \(s,t\) is a counterexample, so \(d(s,t)>5\|st\|_{\tipn}=5-2.5y_0\). Let \(sy\) be the edge of \(s\) in \(C_s^3\). By Lemma 4, \(y\) lies in \(C_t^1\) above \(B_t^1\).

  • The edge: \(\|sy\|_{\hexn}=\|\nabla_s^y\|=1-y_1\), by definition of \(y_1\).
  • The rest of the path: \(y\) is closer to \(t\) than \(s\), so by induction \(d(y,t)\le5\|yt\|_{\tipn}\). Since \(y\) is in the triangle \(C_t^1\cap C_s^3\), whose side is \(1-y_0\), we have \(\|yt\|_{\hexn}\le1-y_0\). Lemma 2 then gives \(\|yt\|_{\tipn}=\|yt\|_{\hexn}-\tfrac12y_1\le1-y_0-\tfrac12y_1\).
$$5-2.5y_0 \;<\; d(s,t) \;\le\; \|sy\|_{\hexn}+d(y,t) \;\le\; (1-y_1)+5\bigl(1-y_0-\tfrac12y_1\bigr),$$

and moving everything to one side gives \(2.5y_0+3.5y_1<1\). The figure shows the value of \(2.5y_0+3.5y_1\) live: when \(y\) is placed where this sum is \(\ge1\), the path through \(y\) is short enough and \(s,t\) cannot be a counterexample.

5.2 The toll for crossing \(R\) (Lemma 9)

Arrows towards \(t\) can circle around it (see the spiral in §2). So the paper only follows a greedy path until its first edge that crosses a boundary ray of \(t\). Since \(R\) is empty, such an edge has to jump over \(R\). Lemma 9 says that it then ends at least twice as close to \(t\) as it is long.

Lemma 9. Let \(uv\) be an edge contained in \(\nabla_u^t\) that crosses both bisectors \(B_t^0\) and \(B_t^1\). Then \(2\|vt\|_{\tipn}\le\|uv\|_{\hexn}\).

The proof in [2] (p. 15) is short and the figure follows it. Take \(u\) in \(C_t^0\) left of \(B_t^0\) and \(v\) in \(C_t^1\) above \(B_t^1\). Let \(v'\) be the point of the horizontal line through \(t\) just above \(v\), and \(t'\) the point where the ray from \(v\) at 240° meets \(B_t^0\).

  • Since \(v\) is above \(B_t^1\), its tipped distance to \(t\) is just its horizontal distance: \(\|vt\|_{\tipn}=|tv'|\).
  • The segment \(vt'\) has slope \(\sqrt3\). So it is twice as long as its horizontal extent: \(|vt'|=2|tv'|\).
  • Since \(u\) is left of \(B_t^0\), the segment \(vt'\) fits in the triangle \(\nabla_u^v\), whose side is \(\|uv\|_{\hexn}\). So \(|vt'|\le\|uv\|_{\hexn}\).

Together: \(2\|vt\|_{\tipn}=|vt'|\le\|uv\|_{\hexn}\). In words, an edge that jumps over the empty region pays a "toll" (the word used in the paper): where it lands, the distance to \(t\) is at most half of its length. Drag \(u\) and \(v\) to try to break it.

5.3 Two candidate paths

The proof compares two ways of going from \(s\) to \(t\). The y-path takes the edge \(sy\) of the cone \(C_s^3\) (the one going up) and then follows greedy routing towards \(t\). The x-path takes the edge \(sx\) of the cone \(C_s^4\) and then follows greedy routing too. In the situation of the proof the y-path goes (roughly) counter-clockwise around \(t\) and the x-path clockwise. The paper explains the idea like this: where the two paths meet they "compete for space": the long edges of one path come with large empty regions that push the other path closer to \(t\) [2, p. 13].

The paper first makes two assumptions (roughly: the greedy path from \(y\) really turns counter-clockwise around \(t\) without getting close to it, and no edge of a certain kind leaves the triangle \(T\) towards cone 4). Then it bounds the length of both paths with variables \(y_i, Y_i, x_i, X_i\). If both paths were longer than the target, these variables would satisfy a list of inequalities (see 5.4).

The figure shows the two paths on a real point set. A counterexample cannot be drawn because it does not exist (that is what the theorem says). Here I follow both greedy paths all the way to \(t\). The proof only follows them until their first edge that crosses \(R_t^0\) and then uses induction. This is where Lemma 9 helps. Drag the points or take a new random set; the turning of each path around \(t\) is measured live.

5.4 The linear programs

The inequalities of 5.3 can never all hold at the same time. This is checked case by case. Under the two assumptions there are \(2\times2\times5=20\) cases. In 16 of them, linear programming shows that the system has no solution. The other 4 need a third, shortcut path (Section 5.3 of [2]). The cases where the assumptions fail are in Sections 5.1 and 5.2 of [2].

This is the part of the proof that surprised me most: the final contradiction is not found by hand but by a computer. I copied the inequalities of Section 4 of [2] (pp. 14, 19 and 20) into my code. The page solves the 20 systems in the browser with a small simplex method (two phases, Bland's rule, in js/lp.js).

I get exactly the result of the paper: 16 systems are infeasible and the 4 feasible ones are (X1, Y1, J2), (X1, Y1, J3), (X1, Y1, J4) and (X0, Y0, J3), the same four cases that the paper handles separately with a third path. I see this as a good sign that I copied the inequalities correctly. I did not re-derive each inequality myself; only (2) is derived in 5.1.

Details: the variables, where each inequality comes from, and how infeasibility is certified

All variables are lengths so they are \(\ge 0\). Roughly, \(Y_i\) is the distance to \(t\) of the first vertex of the y-path in the cone \(C_t^i\) and \(y_i\) measures how far the y-path is from the next cone. \(x_i\) and \(X_i\) are the same for the x-path. \(\|v^yt\|_{\tipn}\) and \(\|v^xt\|_{\tipn}\) are the distances to \(t\) after the first edge of each path that crosses \(R_t^0\). The paper writes two more quantities as sizes of empty triangles, \(\|C_t^i\cap C_{y^i}^{i-2}\|\) and \(\|C_t^i\cap C_{x^i}^{i+2}\|\); I call them \(a_i\) and \(b_i\).

Inequalities (2) and (4)–(7) hold in every case. (6) and (7) say that the y-path and the x-path, completed by induction, are both longer than \(5\|st\|_{\tipn}=5-2.5y_0\). The paper prints them with \(\le\). But they start from the assumption \(d(s,t)>5\|st\|_{\tipn}\). So they also hold with \(<\) and I use them that way. This matters: with \(\le\) only 8 of the 20 systems are infeasible. The 20 cases come from three choices: in which cone the y-path lands after crossing \(R_t^0\) (Y0 or Y1), the same for the x-path (X0 or X1), and how the two paths share the cones (no \(j\), or \(j=2,3,4,5\)).

7Related work

How I searched: I started from the introduction and the reference list of the paper [2], looked up the articles (Crossref, OpenAlex, the publishers' pages) and searched for papers that cite [1]. When I checked (November 2026), OpenAlex did not list any paper citing it yet. This is not surprising for a paper from SoCG 2026. I only keep here the results that are directly about Θ-graphs; the introduction of [2] gives more background (Delaunay triangulations, Yao graphs, other spanners).

Θ-graphs and the undirected case

A geometric graph is a \(c\)-spanner if every shortest path is at most \(c\) times longer than the straight line; the book of Narasimhan and Smid [8] is the standard reference. Θ-graphs come from Clarkson [6] and Keil and Gutwin [7]. For Θ6 the undirected case is well understood. Bonichon, Gavoille, Hanusse and Ilcinkas showed that the undirected Θ6-graph is the union of two TD-Delaunay graphs (the Delaunay graph defined with empty equilateral triangles instead of empty disks), which Chew had shown to be 2-spanners [5], and that 2 is the best possible [11]. More generally, Bose et al. proved the tight bound \(1+2\sin(\theta/2)\) for \(\Theta_{4k+2}\), where \(\theta\) is the angle of the cones [4]; for Θ6 this gives 2 again.

Directed Θ-graphs

With directions things get harder. The directed \(\Theta_k\)-graph is a constant spanner for \(k\ge6\) [3, 4, 9] and for \(k=4\) [10]. Greedy routing gives short paths only for \(k\ge7\) [9]. For Θ6, Akitaya, Biniaz and Bose [3] gave the first bounds: an upper bound of 7 and a construction with 8 points and ratio \(4-\varepsilon\) (their Theorems 7 and 25). Their upper bound starts from a simple fact: if \(ab\) is an edge, the greedy path from \(b\) back to \(a\) is at most \(4\sqrt3\approx 6.9\) times longer than \(ab\) (their Corollary 5). With the bound 2 of the undirected graph this already gives about 13.9. A finer argument through an intermediate vertex brings it down to 7. They also give a routing algorithm that only looks at the neighbours of the current vertex, with routing ratio at most 8. They prove that no such algorithm can do better than \(6-\varepsilon\). They left the gap between 4 and 7 open. The paper [1] closes it with 5. This is the first tight bound for any directed \(\Theta_k\)-graph.

Tight bounds like this are rare. Apart from the \(\Theta_{4k+2}\)-graphs, Table 1 of [2] only lists a few Delaunay-type graphs (with a triangle, a square or a hexagon as empty region). For the usual Delaunay triangulation the exact spanning ratio is still open [2, p. 2].

8Summary and limits

What the page does

  • Builds Θ6 with both definitions, computes exact spanning ratios and greedy routes (§1, §2)
  • Shows that greedy routing can spiral, with an example I built myself (§2)
  • Rebuilds the 5 − ε construction of Lemma 1, checks it numerically and points out a probable typo in the definition of the \(q^i\) (§3)
  • Gives a 5-point example with ratio close to 4, with a slider on δ (§3), a game and a search for bad point sets (§4)
  • Explains the structure of the upper bound proof, with figures for Lemmas 2, 4 and 9 and for the two candidate paths (§5)
  • Solves the 20 linear programs of the paper, finds the same 16 / 4 split and checks a certificate for each infeasible case (§5)
  • Tests the main claims numerically, offline and in the browser (§6)

What I did not do

  • The rest of the upper bound: Sections 5.1–5.3 of [2] (when the assumptions fail, and the third path for the 4 remaining cases). I only describe them.
  • I took the inequalities of the linear programs from the paper. I re-derive the first one, (2), and explain where the other families come from (§5), but I did not re-derive each of them.
  • The 8-point construction of [3] and their spiral figure: I did not reproduce them; my 5-point example and my spiral were built on my own.
  • The \(O(n\log n)\) construction of Θ6 [8]: brute force is fast enough for the sizes used here.

Open questions from my experiments

  • With 5 points I get ratios close to 4 but never above. Is 4 the maximum for 5 points? I do not know a proof.
  • Lemma 1 needs a number of points that grows like \(1/\delta\) to reach \(5-O(\delta)\). Can a smaller point set get closer to 5?
