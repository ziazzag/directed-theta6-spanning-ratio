1Six cones, one arrow per cone

Think of a road network between cities. A road between every pair of cities is far too expensive, so we keep only a few roads, but we still want every trip to be short: never much longer than the straight line. A graph like this is called a spanner, and its worst detour factor is its spanning ratio. Θ-graphs are one of the simplest ways to build one: each city only builds a road to its nearest neighbour in a few directions.

Now make every road one-way. You can still leave a city in every direction, but the road back may not exist, so a trip may have to go around. This is the directed Θ6-graph, and the paper shows that the detour can get as bad as 5 times the straight line, but never worse.

Let \(P\) be a finite set of points in the plane. The three lines of slope \(-\sqrt3\), \(0\) and \(\sqrt3\) through a point \(u\) cut the plane into six cones of 60°, numbered \(C_u^0,\dots,C_u^5\) counter-clockwise, starting with \(C_u^0\) directly below \(u\). In each non-empty cone, \(u\) gets one outgoing edge: to the point whose orthogonal projection on the bisector of the cone is closest to \(u\). Θ-graphs were introduced independently by Clarkson [6] and by Keil and Gutwin [7].

Equivalently, \(u\to v\) is an edge exactly when the canonical triangle \(\nabla_u^v\) (the equilateral triangle with a corner at \(u\), sides parallel to the cone boundaries, and \(v\) on the opposite side) contains no point of \(P\) in its interior. The far side of \(\nabla_u^v\) is exactly the level line of the projection, so the two definitions are the same. My code builds the graph both ways and checks that they agree (§6).

Every vertex has out-degree at most 6, but in-degree can be large. The spanning ratio is

$$\rho(G) = \max_{s\neq t\in P} \frac{d_G(s,t)}{|st|},$$

where \(d_G\) is the length of a shortest directed path. As in the paper, I assume general position: no two points on a line of slope \(0\) or \(\pm\sqrt3\), so that no point lies on a cone boundary.

How it is computed

Brute force: for each \(u\), the closest projection in each cone, \(O(n^2)\). The exact spanning ratio runs Dijkstra from every vertex on the \(\le 6n\) edges, \(O(n^2\log n)\). This is instant for the sizes used here, so I did not implement the \(O(n\log n)\) sweep of [8].

Greedy routing

From the current vertex \(a\), take the out-edge in the cone of \(a\) that contains \(t\). This edge always exists, since \(t\) itself is in that cone. In Θ6 the greedy path can spiral around \(t\) many times while making little progress [2, 3]. Bounded greedy routing is only known for \(k\ge 7\) cones [9].

Things to try

Load 5 points ≈ 4: the points \(s,a,b,c,t\) of the construction below, without the zig-zag, already give 3.966. The ratio tends to 4 as δ → 0. Switch to undirected and see the ratio fall under 2. Compare the greedy path and the shortest path on random sets: they often differ.

3Rebuilding the 5 − ε construction

Lemma 1 of the paper: for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a point set whose directed Θ6-graph has spanning ratio at least \(5-\varepsilon\). Fix \(0<\delta<\varepsilon/72\), \(t=(0,0)\), and put \(s, a, b, c\) close to four corners of the unit hexagon around \(t\):

$$\begin{aligned} s&=(-1,0)+\delta(1,-1), & a&=(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})+\delta(1,-2),\\ b&=(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})+\delta(-2,-3), & c&=(1,0)+\delta(-6,1). \end{aligned}$$

Then two almost parallel lines through \(t\) are added: the points \(p^0,p^1,\dots,p^k\) on the line of slope \(-\delta\) and \(q^0,\dots,q^{k-1}\) on the segment \(ct\). Consecutive points are linked by segments of slope \(\pm(\sqrt3-\delta)\), so they form a zig-zag that gets smaller towards \(t\). From each vertex of the path, all later vertices are in the same cone. So the only way from \(s\) to \(t\) is

s → a → b → c → p⁰ → q⁰ → p¹ → q¹ → ⋯ → pᵏ → t.

The first three edges have length ≈ 1 each. Unfolding the zig-zag gives the key step: its length equals \(|p^0r|+|rp^k|\), where \(r\approx(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})\). So the zig-zag is as long as going from \(c\) back up to the top of the hexagon and down again to \(t\). In total this is ≈ 5, while \(|st|\le 1\). The paper bounds the total from below by \(5-72\delta\).

What the numbers say

For every δ I tried, Dijkstra finds exactly the path described in the paper, and the ratio is above \(5-72\delta\). The measured gap is about \(5-\text{ratio}\approx 18\,\delta\) (for example 4.9818 at δ = 0.001, with 5 962 points). The constant 72 of the paper is therefore not tight, which is expected since each length is bounded separately.

The number of points grows like \(1/\delta\): the zig-zag steps shrink geometrically towards \(t\), and the construction stops when \(p^k\) gets closer to \(t\) than δ. Approaching 5 is expensive. By contrast, 5 points (\(s,a,b,c,t\)) already give a ratio close to 4 (see the game below).

Implementation note: a probable typo in the paper. The full version [2] (p. 5) defines the points \(q^i\) by \(\|q^{i-1}q^i\|_2/\|tq^i\|_2 = \|q^0c\|_2/\|tc\|_2\). Built literally, this point set has the wrong shortest path: it jumps \(p^0\to p^1\to\cdots\), skips about half of the points, and the ratio stays below 4 (3.95 at \(\delta=0.005\)). With the denominator \(\|tq^{i-1}\|_2\) instead, \((q^{i-1},p^i,q^i)\) is a scaled copy of \((c,p^0,q^0)\), the formula also holds for \(i=0\) (with \(q^{-1}=c\)), and the shortest path is exactly the one of Figure 2. This page uses that version, and my test suite compares both. Still to be confirmed.

First results of the random search

I wrote a simulated-annealing search: it moves one point at a time, and accepts worse moves with a probability that decreases over time. Moves that bring two points within \(10^{-3}\) of each other, or within \(10^{-3}\) rad of a cone boundary, are rejected. Without this rule, the search exploits degenerate configurations (points on the same horizontal line) and reports meaningless ratios in the thousands. 5Why never more than 5: a map of the proof

The paper proves something stronger than \(d(s,t)\le 5|st|\):

Theorem 1. For every \(P\) in general position and all \(s,t\in P\): \(d_{\Theta_6}(s,t)\le 5\|st\|_{\tipn}\le 5|st|\).

The idea is to measure distances with hexagons instead of circles, because hexagons fit the six cones. Here \(\|\cdot\|_{\hexn}\) is the hexagonal norm whose unit ball is the regular hexagon with corners \((\pm1,0)\) (it defines Θ6), and \(\|\cdot\|_{\tipn}\) is the "tipped" hexagonal norm whose unit ball has corners \((0,\pm 2/\sqrt3)\). We have \(\|\cdot\|_{\tipn}\le\|\cdot\|_2\le\|\cdot\|_{\hexn}\).

  1. Induction on \(\|st\|_{\hexn}\). Assume the bound holds for every pair that is closer than \(s\) and \(t\), and look for a short enough path from \(s\) to \(t\). The closest pair is a direct edge. Normalise \(\|st\|_{\hexn}=1\) and, by symmetry, \(s\in C_t^0\) to the right of the bisector \(B_t^0\).
  2. Lemma 2. The target length can be read off one small triangle between \(s\) and \(t\): the bigger the triangle, the smaller the target. Let \(y_0\) be the side of the triangle \(T=C_t^0\cap C_s^2\). Then \(1=\|st\|_{\tipn}+\tfrac12 y_0\), so the target is \(5\|st\|_{\tipn}=5-2.5\,y_0\). checked numerically
  3. Empty region (Def. 1, Lemma 4). If \(s,t\) were a counterexample, a whole region between them would have to be empty, and the arrow leaving \(s\) upwards would have to land in a small window. Let \(p=B_s^3\cap B_t^1\) and \(q=B_s^4\cap B_t^0\), and let \(R\) be the parallelogram \(sqtp\) together with \(\nabla_s^p\) and \(\nabla_s^q\). If \(d(s,t)>5\|st\|_{\tipn}\), then no point is inside \(R\), the edge \(sy\) of \(C_s^3\) ends in \(C_t^1\), and \(2.5y_0+3.5y_1<1\), where \(y_1=1-\|\nabla_s^y\|\). figure
  4. Greedy paths and spirals. Arrows towards \(t\) can circle around it, so the paper only follows them for a short stretch. The greedy path towards \(t\) may spiral, so the paper follows it only until its first edge crossing a boundary ray of \(t\). An edge that crosses both \(B_t^0\) and \(B_t^1\) ends at least twice as close to \(t\) as it is long (Lemma 9). figure planned
  5. Two candidate paths. There are two natural ways to reach \(t\), one on each side. If both were too long, the positions of the points would have to satisfy a list of inequalities. The y-path goes counter-clockwise around \(t\), the x-path clockwise. Their lengths give a system of linear inequalities in \(y_0, y_1, \dots\) to explain
  6. Linear programming. These inequalities can never all hold at the same time, and this is checked case by case. Under two assumptions there are \(2\times2\times5=20\) cases. In 16 of them, linear programming shows that the system has no solution. The other 4 need a third, shortcut path. The cases where the assumptions fail are handled in the full version. reproduce one LP in the page
